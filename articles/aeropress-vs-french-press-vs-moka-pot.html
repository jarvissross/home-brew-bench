What actually differs: five questions

1. What kind of cup do you want?

The French press leaves its metal mesh filter in place, so oils and some fine particles stay in the cup. Our French press guide covers how pressure from a plunger and filter design change sediment, and why some presses add a second filter. The AeroPress's paper filter holds oils back, which is why brewers describe its cup as cleaner. A moka pot gives a short, strong coffee. It is often described as espresso-like, but with roughly 1 to 2 bars of pressure against about 9 for espresso, it isn't espresso.

2. How many people are you making coffee for?

A 34 oz French press fills several mugs in one go. The AeroPress makes about one mug per press, so serving four takes four presses. A 3-cup moka pot, at 4.6 oz in America's Test Kitchen's measurement, is one small serving; a 6-cup pot suits two people or a larger milk drink. See our moka pot guide for sizes.

3. How much do you care about water temperature and recipe control?

The AeroPress gives you the most control: you choose the water temperature, steep time and amount, and AeroPress's published recipes range from dark roasts at 180 to 190°F to light roasts at 205 to 210°F. A French press has fewer variables (grind, ratio, time). A moka pot has the fewest, because the stove sets the temperature.

4. How often will you clean it, and how patient are you?

The AeroPress is the quickest to clean according to its maker, and the moka pot needs the most disassembly. The AeroPress blog also warns that a moka pot with a clogged safety valve from poor cleaning could be dangerous, so wash it and check the valve. That is the brand's caution and we haven't found a second source.

5. Where will you use it?

Both the AeroPress and the French press need only hot water, which makes them good at the office. A moka pot needs a stove and, if you have induction, a stainless pot made for it (see our moka guide). The AeroPress Go is built to travel: AeroPress gives its packed weight as 11.4 oz in a mug it packs into. Our travel coffee maker guide compares it with other portable brewers.

Whichever you buy, the grinder matters more

All three brewers work best with freshly ground beans at the right size: coarse for a French press, medium-coarse for a moka pot, and flexible for an AeroPress. A blade grinder can't hold one size, which is why our burr vs blade explainer comes before our grinders under $200 and manual grinder guides. Store the beans in an airtight canister, and a scale makes any of the three more repeatable.

FAQ

Is AeroPress coffee the same as espresso?

No. AeroPress's own blog says the AeroPress uses about 2 bars of manual pressure, and that a moka pot uses about 1 to 2 bars of steam pressure, against about 9 bars for real espresso. Both make a strong, concentrated cup that works for lattes or Americanos, but neither makes true espresso.

Which is cheapest?

At the example prices above, the French press and the AeroPress were both around $40, and the Moka Express a little more. Each category has cheaper and pricier models, so compare on the day you buy.

Which is best for beginners?

The French press has the simplest idea (steep, then press), but it needs an even, coarse grind. The AeroPress is more forgiving about the recipe once you have a timer. The moka pot is the easiest to get wrong, because the stove sets the temperature.

Can I use pre-ground coffee?

You can, but the right grind size differs between all three brewers, so pre-ground coffee is usually a compromise. If you can afford one upgrade, make it a burr grinder.
