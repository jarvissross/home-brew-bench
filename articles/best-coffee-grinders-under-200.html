Grinding your beans right before you brew is the single biggest upgrade most home coffee drinkers can make, and the grinder matters more than the brewer. The good news is that under $200 you can get a real burr grinder that makes even grounds. The catch is that the grinders in this range are built for different jobs. One that's great for pour-over may not grind fine enough for espresso, and an espresso-focused one can be overkill for a drip machine.

This guide compares seven grinders that come up again and again in buying guides for this price range. For each, it lays out the specs and the tradeoffs, then names a pick for each brew method.

How to choose: three questions

1. What do you brew most?

Drip machines, pour-over, AeroPress and French press all use medium to coarse grinds. Almost any grinder here will handle them. What separates the grinders is how consistent the grounds are and how easy it is to repeat a setting. Espresso needs a much finer grind and very small adjustments between settings, because a tiny change in grind size changes the shot a lot. That's the main reason the Encore ESP and Smart Grinder Pro exist alongside the regular Encore.

2. Electric or manual?

Manual grinders put better burrs in a cheaper, smaller package, but you hand-crank every dose and they hold only about one or two cups' worth at a time. If you make coffee for more than one person, or you're never going to grind by hand at 6 a.m., buy electric.

3. Conical or flat burrs?

Every grinder in this guide uses conical burrs except possibly the Cuisinart, whose burr shape retailers don't list. Coffee guides generally describe conical burrs as quieter and more forgiving, and flat burrs as producing very uniform particles with more "clarity" in the cup. Under $200 the burr shape matters less than the build quality and how fine the grinder can adjust.

The grinders in detail

Baratza Encore: best all-rounder for filter coffee

The Encore has been a default beginner recommendation in coffee guides for years, and the specs show why it's popular. It has 40mm conical steel burrs and 40 stepped settings, which one retailer puts at roughly 250–1200 microns. You set the grind by turning the hopper. Baratza's spec sheet lists a 225 g bean hopper and a 142 g grounds bin. Seattle Coffee Gear publishes a rough settings guide: about 8–11 for AeroPress, 13–14 for V60-style pour-over, 20–21 for Chemex, and 30–32 for French press.

Pros
  • Covers every filter brew method with clear starting settings
  • Simple controls, easy to set up
  • Lowest price of the Baratza electric models here
Cons
  • The big steps between settings make espresso hard to dial in
  • Reviewers call it loud
  • One guide reports that the grind setting resets when the hopper is removed

Buy it if you brew drip, pour-over or French press and want a grinder you won't outgrow soon. Check price

Baratza Encore ESP: best for espresso plus filter

The ESP has the same 40mm footprint as the Encore but splits the dial in two. Settings 1–20 use fine micro-steps for dialing in espresso, and settings 21–40 use larger steps so you can jump quickly to filter grinds. It uses M2 steel conical burrs driven by a DC motor. At $199.95 at Seattle Coffee Gear, it's at the top of this budget. Baratza also sells a newer, stepless "ESP Pro" for about $299. One reviewer felt the original ESP was the better value, and the Pro is above this guide's price limit anyway.

Pros
  • Designed for espresso, but still handles pour-over and French press
  • Micro-steps in the espresso range
Cons
  • About $50 more than the regular Encore at the retailer we checked
  • Hopper and bin capacities differ between retailer listings

Buy it if you own or plan to buy an espresso machine but still make filter coffee some days. Check price

Fellow Opus: best for pour-over enthusiasts

The Opus launched at $195. Retailers list 6-blade 40mm conical burrs, 6 Nm of torque, a 350 RPM burr speed, 41+ stepped settings with a printed grind guide, and a micro-adjustment ring inside the grinder around the lower burr for finer tuning. Its 110 g capacity is small next to the Encore's 225 g, but it's plenty if you brew a cup or two at a time.

Pros
  • Fine-tuning ring on top of the stepped settings
  • Compact, design-forward look
  • Retailer lists a 2-year manufacturer's warranty
Cons
  • Small bean capacity (110 g)
  • One retailer says it's not designed specifically for espresso and that its espresso adjustment range is limited
  • Reviewers report mixed results from the anti-static feature, and one reports jamming at the finest setting

Buy it if pour-over and AeroPress are your main brews, you want fine control in that range, and countertop looks matter to you. Check price

Breville Smart Grinder Pro: best for entry-level espresso machine owners

The Smart Grinder Pro (model BCG820) has the most features here. It has 60 grind settings, an LCD screen, and "Dosing iQ" grind-time control in 0.2-second steps. It comes with two portafilter cradles so you can grind straight into the basket. Retailer listings describe 40mm stainless conical burrs at 450 RPM and a 165 W motor. Coffee Review cites a $199.95 MSRP, and some international listings run higher.

Pros
  • Grinds directly into a portafilter, with timed dosing
  • 60 settings cover espresso through French press
  • Large hopper (listed at roughly 16–18 oz)
Cons
  • Larger footprint than the Baratza and Fellow models
  • More electronics and features than a filter-only brewer needs

Buy it if you have a home espresso machine and want timed, hands-off dosing into the portafilter. Check price

OXO Brew Conical Burr Grinder: best simple grinder for drip

OXO's own listing describes stainless steel conical burrs, 15 grind settings plus additional micro-settings, and a one-touch timer that remembers your last setting. The 12 oz UV-blocking hopper is big enough to hold a typical bag of beans. Seattle Coffee Gear also lists a locking shutoff valve on the hopper and a stainless steel grounds container. Note that some third-party sites quote 42 or 43 settings, which doesn't match OXO's official count.

Pros
  • Timer you can set and forget, good for daily drip
  • Large hopper holds a full bag of beans
  • One reviewer found it quieter than most competitors
Cons
  • Not suitable for espresso
  • One reviewer notes the grounds container doesn't lock securely

Buy it if you brew the same pot of drip coffee every morning and want to press one button. Check price

Timemore Chestnut C3: best on a budget (manual)

The C3 puts 38mm stainless conical burrs, in Timemore's "Spike to Cut" (S2C) design, inside an all-metal body with dual bearings. It holds about 20–25 g per grind, which is roughly one or two cups. One review site cites a price of about $99. Newer C3s and C3s MAX versions exist, and the MAX holds about 30 g and has a folding handle.

Pros
  • Metal build with dual-bearing burr stability at a low price
  • Portable, silent, no outlet needed
Cons
  • Small capacity means hand-cranking every dose
  • One review calls fine-tuning "ballpark," and another reports it's slower than its predecessor at medium-coarse settings

Buy it if you brew for one, want better grind quality per dollar than budget electrics, or travel and camp with your coffee. Check price

Cuisinart DBM-8 Supreme Grind: cheapest electric burr option

The DBM-8 is the budget electric choice. Retailers list 18 grind positions, a 4-to-18-cup quantity dial, an 8 oz removable hopper, a grind chamber that holds enough for 32 cups, and automatic shut-off. A third-party coffee guide measured its range at roughly 420–1400 microns. That puts its finest setting near medium-fine, which works for auto-drip but not espresso.

Pros
  • Lowest-cost electric burr grinder in this guide
  • Cup-quantity dial makes batch drip easy
Cons
  • Can't grind fine enough for espresso
  • Retailers don't list the burr type, and listed weights conflict
  • A clear step down in adjustability from everything above

Buy it if you're upgrading from a blade grinder, you only brew auto-drip, and budget is the priority. Check price

FAQ

Is a burr grinder really better than a blade grinder?

For consistency, yes. Coffee guides explain that blade grinders chop beans at random and leave a mix of fine dust and large chunks, while burrs crush beans between two surfaces into much more even particles. Even grounds extract evenly, which is where the flavor difference comes from. Our burr vs blade explainer covers it in detail.

Can I make espresso with the regular Baratza Encore?

Retailer settings guides list an espresso range (about 2–6) on the Encore. But the steps between settings are large, which makes dialing in a shot hard. If espresso matters to you, the Encore ESP's micro-steps exist for this reason.

How fine should I grind for pour-over?

Medium-fine. As a starting point, Seattle Coffee Gear suggests settings 13–14 on the Baratza Encore for a V60-style brewer and 20–21 for a Chemex. Then adjust by taste: grind finer if the coffee tastes sour or thin, coarser if it tastes bitter or the water drains slowly.

Manual or electric for one person?

A manual grinder like the Timemore C3 gives strong grind quality per dollar if you don't mind cranking 20–25 g by hand. If you'd skip fresh grinding on busy mornings, an electric grinder is worth the extra cost.
