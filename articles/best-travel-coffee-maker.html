How to choose a travel coffee maker

1. Where you'll use it

Hotels and flights: small, no cord, nothing that leaks in a bag. An AeroPress Go or a Nanopresso fits here. Car camping: weight barely matters, so a bigger press for several people makes sense. Backpacking: every ounce counts, which is where a collapsible dripper wins. Commuting: a press that is also your mug saves a step.

2. What you like to drink

Clean, full cups: AeroPress or a dripper. Heavy, French-press body: the Bodum or GSI presses. A short, strong shot for espresso drinks: one of the Wacaco pumps. None of these steams milk; see our milk frother guide if you want lattes at home.

3. One cup or several

Most travel brewers make one cup. The AeroPress Go Plus fills its 16 oz tumbler in two pressings. Only the 30 oz JavaPress makes coffee for a group in one brew.

4. Grinder and water

Pre-ground coffee works in all of these, but grind matters most for the Picopresso, which uses a regular (non-pressurized) 52 mm basket and needs a fine, even espresso grind. If you grind on the road, a hand grinder is the usual partner: see our manual grinder guide. For hot water at home or at camp, a gooseneck kettle gives the most control with a dripper.

5. Cleanup

The AeroPress pushes out a dry puck and AeroPress lists the Go Plus as dishwasher safe. French presses leave wet grounds you have to dump somewhere, which is a real chore in a hotel room or on a trail. Mesh drippers need rinsing after each use.

The 7 brewers in detail

1. AeroPress Go: best for most travelers

The AeroPress Go is the compact version of the AeroPress. It brews one 8 oz cup, and the whole kit (chamber, plunger, filter cap, stirrer, scoop, paper filters and a lid) packs inside its own cup. AeroPress gives the packed weight as 11.4 oz (323 g) including the accessories and 20 paper filters, and the packed size as 4.5" tall by 3.6" wide. The body is polypropylene with a silicone cup lid.

It shows up near the top of most 2026 travel brewer roundups we looked at, often described as the default pick. The appeal is that it's small, has no glass to break, makes a clean cup with paper filters, and cleans up by pushing out a puck of grounds.

Pros: packs into its own mug; light; no glass; easy cleanup; paper filters are tiny to carry.
Cons: one cup per press; you need to carry paper filters; the included polypropylene cup is a plain cup, not an insulated tumbler like the Go Plus's.

2. AeroPress Go Plus: best with an insulated tumbler

The Go Plus pairs a larger 10 oz brewer with a double-wall, vacuum-insulated 18/8 stainless steel tumbler that holds up to 16 oz, so two pressings fill it. AeroPress lists the chamber, plunger and filter cap as Tritan, the tumbler lid as Tritan with a silicone gasket and an embedded magnet, the packed size as 7.3" x 4.2", and the packed weight as 1 lb 4 oz (0.56 kg). It's listed as dishwasher safe. At $89.95 it costs about $40 more than the Go (AeroPress, Oct 20, 2026).

Pros: keeps coffee hot; bigger brew; everything nests in the tumbler; dishwasher safe.
Cons: nearly twice the packed weight of the Go; costs much more; a full tumbler means pressing twice.

3. Wacaco Nanopresso: budget espresso-style shots

The Nanopresso is a hand-pumped espresso maker: you add ground coffee and hot water, then pump to build pressure. Wacaco rates it at up to 18 bar, with an 80 ml water tank, an 8 g dose in a 39 mm basket inside a pressurized portafilter, and an optional 16 g double basket sold separately. It weighs 336 g (11.9 oz), measures 156 x 71 x 62 mm, and comes with a built-in cup, scoop, brush and cloth pouch. Wacaco notes it doesn't heat water.

A pressurized basket is more forgiving of grind size than a standard espresso basket, which makes the Nanopresso easier to use with pre-ground coffee. The tradeoff is less control than the Picopresso.

Pros: espresso-style shot anywhere; forgiving; built-in cup; costs about half the Picopresso.
Cons: small 8 g single dose unless you buy the double basket; pumping takes effort; one shot at a time.

4. Wacaco Picopresso: for espresso enthusiasts

The Picopresso is Wacaco's more serious model. It takes an 18 g dose in a 52 mm double basket, with the same 18 bar maximum and 80 ml tank as the Nanopresso. It's more compact (71 x 78 x 106 mm) and weighs 350 g (12.35 oz). Wacaco includes a tamper, distribution tool, funnel, scoop, brush and a protective case. Like the Nanopresso, it doesn't heat water.

Because the basket isn't pressurized, results depend on a fine, consistent grind, so this is a pick for people who already make espresso at home and will travel with a capable grinder.

Pros: full 18 g double dose; compact; tamper and case included.
Cons: $129.90 (Wacaco, Oct 20, 2026); much less forgiving of grind; more steps and more to clean.

5. Bodum Travel Press (stainless steel): best for commuters

The Travel Press is a French press built into a double-wall travel mug: add coffee and hot water, put the lid on, wait, and press. Home Grounds reviewed the stainless 15 oz version (updated Jan 26, 2025). It found that the 15 oz size brews about 13 oz and the 12 oz size about 10 oz, that the mug kept coffee above 120°F for about two hours, and that the parts can go in the dishwasher. Bodum also sells a cheaper double-wall plastic version, which Home Grounds didn't test.

Home Grounds' main complaints: because the grounds stay in the mug, "the last few sips are likely to be over-extracted and bitter"; the mug isn't leakproof because the plunger passes through the lid; and you can't easily stir in milk after brewing.

Pros: brew and drink from one container; insulated; dishwasher safe; inexpensive.
Cons: coffee gets stronger and more bitter as you drink; not leakproof, so not for a bag; one cup.

6. GSI Outdoors JavaPress (30 oz): best for camping with a group

A lightweight plastic French press for camp. CleverHiker (June 22, 2025) lists it at 30 fl oz and 10.2 oz, rates it 4.2/5 and names it "Best Coffee Maker for Groups". It praises the hands-off brewing (you can do other camp chores while it steeps), the light weight for its capacity, and how easy it is to adjust strength, calling it "a user-friendly coffee maker that performs equally well at the campground and the kitchen."

The downsides, per CleverHiker: it's hard to clean and awkward to pack, and it "ranks low for portability compared to other coffee makers designed for camping." GSI also sells a smaller 20 oz Personal JavaPress designed to nest with a mug.

Pros: several cups at once; light for its size; hands-off; no paper filters.
Cons: bulky to pack; messy to clean at camp; French-press sediment.

7. GSI Outdoors Ultralight Java Drip: lightest for backpacking

A small collapsible pour-over cone with a built-in nylon mesh filter, so there are no paper filters to carry. OutdoorGearLab weighed it at 1.1 oz with its included cup and said the dripper alone is "less than half an ounce"; it folds small enough to fit under a fuel canister. Their verdict: "A great ultralight backpacking option, as long as you pour carefully and aren't too rough with the contents of your pack."

Their criticisms: you "must pour carefully" for good flavor (harder from a stove pot than from a gooseneck kettle), the clips are delicate, it's finicky with some cup rims, and the mesh needs extra cleaning after each use. Note that OutdoorGearLab's review dates from 2018, so check the current product page for changes.

Pros: by far the lightest and smallest; no filters to buy; cheap.
Cons: technique-sensitive; delicate clips; one cup at a time; older review data.

FAQ

Can I take a travel coffee maker on a plane?

Brewers like the AeroPress and Wacaco pumps are just plastic and metal parts, but security rules decide what's allowed, and they can change. Check your airline and your country's airport security agency before you fly, and pack the brewer empty and dry.

Do any of these heat water?

No. Wacaco states plainly that both the Nanopresso and Picopresso need hot water added, and the AeroPress, French presses and dripper all rely on you bringing hot water.

AeroPress Go or Go Plus?

The Go is lighter (11.4 oz vs 1 lb 4 oz packed, per AeroPress) and cheaper. Pick the Go Plus if you want a bigger brew and an insulated tumbler that keeps coffee hot.

Nanopresso or Picopresso?

The Nanopresso's pressurized basket is easier with pre-ground coffee and it costs about half as much. The Picopresso takes a bigger 18 g dose and rewards a good grinder, but is less forgiving.

What about plug-in single-serve machines?

Compact pod and drip machines exist, but they need an outlet and a flat surface, and most aren't small enough for a carry-on. This guide sticks to unpowered brewers.
