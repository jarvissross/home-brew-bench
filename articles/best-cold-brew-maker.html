Cold brew is about the easiest coffee there is to make. You steep coarse grounds in cold water for many hours, then filter them out. You could do it in any jar with a sieve. A dedicated cold brew maker makes the filtering step cleaner and the batch easier to store, and some let you choose between a strong concentrate and a ready-to-drink brew.

The real differences between models come down to four things: how much they make, whether they're built for concentrate or drinkable coffee, how they filter (mesh, felt or paper), and whether the container fits in your fridge. There's also a small class of electric brewers that skip the overnight wait. This guide compares eight options across all of those.

What actually matters in a cold brew maker

Concentrate or ready-to-drink

Most cold brew makers are built around a concentrate: a high ratio of coffee to water that you dilute with water or milk when you pour a glass (roughly half and half is a common starting point, adjusted to taste). Concentrate takes up less fridge space per serving. The OXO and Toddy are designed for it. Others, like the Hario Mizudashi at its suggested recipe (80 g of coffee to 1000 ml of water), make coffee you can drink straight. Pitchers like the Takeya, Bodum and mason jar can do either. It just depends on how much coffee you put in.

Batch size and fridge fit

Think about how many glasses you drink a week. Food Network lists the OXO at 24–28 oz of concentrate (12–14 drinks, at 2 oz per drink) and the Toddy at 36–38 oz (up to 19 drinks). Takeya says its 2-quart concentrate makes about 18 servings once diluted. Then check the shape: tall pitchers fit in a fridge door, while wide, stand-mounted brewers like the OXO (about 9.5 × 9.5 × 14.7 inches in its listing) usually brew on the counter, and you move only the carafe to the fridge. Reviewed.com's testers found the Takeya's shape awkward in their fridge, so measure your shelf height.

Filter type: mesh, felt or paper

Stainless and polyester mesh filters are reusable and easy to rinse, but they let more fine particles through, so the coffee has a little more body and sometimes some sediment. Felt (Toddy) and paper filters catch more fines for a cleaner cup, but they drain more slowly and need replacing over time. The OXO takes optional paper filters if you want both.

Glass vs plastic

Glass (Hario, OXO carafe, mason jar) doesn't hold onto smells and lets you see the brew, but it can break. Tritan and other BPA-free plastics (Takeya, Bodum) are lighter and tougher. County Line's listing warns against adding warm or boiling water, so don't use hot-bloom recipes in it.

Electric "fast" cold brewers

Electric brewers like the Cuisinart DCB-10 and DASH Rapid cut the wait from overnight to under an hour. They cost more, take counter space, and make ready-to-drink coffee rather than a concentrate. Food Network's testers also found the DASH quite loud. They're for people who decide on cold brew the same morning they want it.

FAQ

How long should cold brew steep?

Most steeped cold brew recipes call for roughly 12 to 24 hours. Hario's Mizudashi recipe at Prima Coffee uses 18 hours in the fridge. Electric brewers like the Cuisinart DCB-10 take 25 to 45 minutes. Longer steeps make a stronger brew, so adjust to taste.

What's a good coffee-to-water ratio for cold brew?

It depends on whether you want concentrate or drinkable coffee. Prima Coffee's Mizudashi recipe (80 g of coffee to 1000 ml of water, about 1:12.5) makes a drinkable brew. Concentrate recipes use a lot more coffee for the same water, and you dilute the result when you serve it. Follow your maker's instructions first, then adjust.

How long does cold brew last in the fridge?

Takeya says concentrate keeps for up to two weeks in its airtight pitcher. Flavor fades over time in any container, so many people brew smaller batches more often. Keep it covered and refrigerated.

Is cold brew the same as iced coffee?

No. Iced coffee is usually hot-brewed coffee that's chilled or poured over ice. Cold brew is never heated: it extracts slowly in cold water, which is why it takes hours.

Do I need a special cold brew maker?

No. A jar and a fine strainer or paper filter work. A dedicated maker mainly makes filtering cleaner and storing easier. That's why the cheapest options here (the Mizudashi and Bodum) do most of what the pricier ones do.
