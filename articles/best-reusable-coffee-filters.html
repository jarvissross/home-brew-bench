A reusable filter saves you buying paper, but it changes the cup: more oils and body, sometimes some sediment, and more cleaning. There is also a health point most roundups skip: coffee oils contain cafestol, which can raise LDL cholesterol, and paper is what removes most of it. This guide covers the metal and cloth options named in two published roundups, explains the trade-offs, and says where the sources are thin. If you would rather stay with paper, see our paper filter guide.

The cholesterol catch

This is the main reason to think before dropping paper. Cafestol is a compound in coffee oil. According to NutritionFacts.org's summary of the research, paper filters remove most of it, and switching from a French press or metal-mesh coffee maker to a paper filter can cut more than 90 percent of the cafestol. The same page says it is presumably as high with metal-mesh drip as with French press unless paper is added, but notes it did not test that.

A 2026 Swedish study of 14 workplace coffee machines, as reported by a science news site (we read a search summary, not the paper), found a median cafestol level of 176 mg/L in coffee from machines with metal filters versus 12 mg/L in paper-filtered coffee, with large variation between machines. The researchers' LDL figure is a modelled estimate, not a measured result. We found no source testing home metal cones or cloth filters for cafestol, so how much a given cone lets through is unknown. If you have high cholesterol, ask a doctor; otherwise, a paper-lined brewer or occasional metal use is the cautious reading of the sources. The effect depends on how much you drink, and the figures above come from studies of daily intake, not an occasional cup.

What matters when choosing

  • Fit first. Chemex, V60, carafe rim, basket and cloth filters are not interchangeable. Match the listing's named brewer.
  • Mesh and layers. The roundup's author argues fit and mesh density matter more than brand. Finer or double mesh catches more sediment and flows slower; single layer flows faster and lets more through.
  • Grind. Several sources say go coarser for tight mesh (MISETTO) and finer for single layer (Able KONE). That is the opposite direction for each, so follow the maker's guidance. See our pour-over grinder guide if your grinder lacks range.
  • Cleaning. Metal needs a brush or rinse after each use; some are dishwasher safe. Cloth must be rinsed, boiled periodically per the Pinyon notes, and dried or stored properly. Storage advice differs: Complete Home Barista says to store cloth wet to prevent mold, while the Pinyon notes in the roundup say it must dry fully between uses. Follow the maker's instructions.
  • Durability claims. "Replaces 25,000 paper filters" (Barista Warrior) is a manufacturer claim reported by the roundup, not a measurement.
  • Cost. A paper filter costs roughly 3 to 11 cents each in our paper filter guide (our division of Chowhound's pack prices). Reusables have no prices in our main source, so we cannot say how many brews it takes to break even.

FAQ

Do reusable filters make better coffee?

Different, not better. Sources describe a fuller body from metal with some fines, a cleaner cup from cloth, and a cleanest cup from paper.

Is a metal filter bad for cholesterol?

The research we read points that way for unfiltered and metal-filtered coffee, with the size of the effect depending on how much you drink. We found no study on home cone filters specifically.

Can I use paper and a metal mesh together?

We found no source that tests this. Some people line a cone with paper, which would take you back to paper's behavior; we can't say more.

How do I clean a cloth filter?

Rinse after each use; the Pinyon notes mention boiling clean in 3 to 5 minutes. Follow the maker's instructions.
