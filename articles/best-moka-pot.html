A moka pot brews strong, concentrated coffee on the stove. Water in the bottom chamber heats up, steam pressure pushes it up through a basket of ground coffee, and the coffee collects in the top chamber. It isn't true espresso (the pressure is far lower than an espresso machine's), but it's an inexpensive way to get a small, intense cup that works for lattes with a milk frother. The two decisions that matter most are your stove (classic aluminum pots don't work on induction) and size (a moka pot only works well when filled to its rated size).

What matters when choosing a moka pot

1. Your stove: aluminum or stainless steel

Aluminum is the traditional material, heats quickly and is usually cheaper, but it is not magnetic, so it won't work on an induction hob. Stainless steel pots (or pots with a steel base, like the Bialetti Moka Induction) work on induction as well as gas and electric. America's Test Kitchen says the choice of material doesn't affect performance; stainless is mainly about induction. If you own an aluminum pot and switch to induction, Bialetti sells an adapter plate ($29.95 at Sur La Table, Oct 19, 2026).

2. Size: buy the size you'll actually brew

Unlike a French press, a moka pot isn't designed to be half-filled: you fill the basket and the water to the valve. A 3-cup pot makes about one mug-sized drink's worth of strong coffee for one person (4.6 oz in ATK's testing of the Moka Express). A 6-cup suits two people or a bigger latte. Some brands sell reducer baskets for brewing smaller amounts in a big pot. A reader on Slickdeals noted that the Cuisinox Roma's 6-cup reducer is sold separately, so check before you rely on one.

3. Design details that reviewers noticed

America's Test Kitchen preferred broad bases (more contact with the heat and more stable on gas grates), wide openings on the center tube (less spurting), curved handles that fit the hand, and a safety valve you can see, because the valve marks the maximum fill line. Honest Coffee Guide found its best-tasting pots had a basket-to-lower-chamber volume ratio of about 3:10, and that pots with shallow baskets made watery coffee.

4. Cleaning and parts

Most moka pots are hand-wash only. Sur La Table lists the Moka Express, Venus and Brikka as hand wash, and John Lewis's Brikka listing says to wash it with water only, without soap or detergent. The rubber gasket and filter plate wear out over time, so a brand with easy-to-find spare parts matters. Honest Coffee Guide counted easy-to-find parts as a plus for the Bialetti range.

5. Grind

Moka pots need a grind finer than drip but coarser than espresso. A burr grinder makes this easier to dial in. See our espresso grinder guide (most of those grinders also cover moka grinds), grinders under $200, or manual grinders.

Bialetti Moka Express: best for most people

The eight-sided aluminum Moka Express is the design most people picture when they hear "moka pot", and it comes in several sizes (Sur La Table lists 3 and 6 cups; other retailers carry more). America's Test Kitchen named it the winner of its moka pot review (updated July 2025), calling it "the most reliable and user-friendly moka pot". Pros: top pick of several independent reviewers; several sizes; spare gaskets and filters are easy to find; light and quick to heat. Cons: not induction-compatible; aluminum can discolor or oxidize; the handle can get warm (Homes & Gardens).

How to get better coffee from a moka pot

  1. Fill the bottom chamber with water up to, not over, the safety valve. Some people start with hot water to shorten the time the grounds sit on the heat; America's Test Kitchen notes the water can easily overheat past about 205°F in the brew bed.
  2. Fill the basket with ground coffee and level it without tamping.
  3. Screw the top on firmly and set the pot on medium heat, with the handle pointing away from a gas flame.
  4. Take it off the heat as soon as the coffee starts sputtering into the top chamber, as ATK advises. Some people cool the base under the tap to stop extraction.
  5. Rinse and dry the parts after each use, and replace the gasket when it hardens or leaks.

FAQ

Is moka pot coffee espresso?

Not quite. A moka pot uses steam pressure to push water through the coffee, which is much less pressure than an espresso machine uses. The result is strong and concentrated, and it's commonly used the same way as espresso in lattes and other milk drinks.

Can I use a moka pot on an induction stove?

Only if it's made for it: a stainless steel pot like the London Sip or Bialetti Venus, or a pot with a steel base like the Bialetti Moka Induction. Classic aluminum pots such as the Moka Express, Grosche Milano and standard Brikka need an adapter plate.

Aluminum or stainless steel: which makes better coffee?

America's Test Kitchen says the material doesn't affect performance. Choose on stove type, weight and care: aluminum is lighter and cheaper; stainless works on induction and doesn't discolor the same way.

Can I put a moka pot in the dishwasher?

Usually not. Sur La Table lists the Moka Express, Venus and Brikka as hand wash only. John Lewis says the Moka Induction's boiler can go in the dishwasher but the other parts should be hand-washed.

Can I brew less than the full amount?

Moka pots are designed to be filled to their rated size, which is why size matters when you buy. Some brands sell reducer baskets for smaller batches; check whether one exists for your model.

Is there an electric moka pot?

Yes. America's Test Kitchen's "Best Electric" pick was the Bodum Chambord 12 oz Electric Espresso Maker, which brewed full-flavored coffee quickly without needing a stove. Its downsides were less body than the stovetop pots and a hidden valve.
