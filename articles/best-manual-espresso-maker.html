"Manual espresso maker" here means a device that makes espresso with no wall outlet: you supply the hot water (from a kettle, or from a built-in battery heater on two models) and the pressure, by lever, pump or piston. They suit small kitchens, travel, camping and anyone curious about espresso without buying a full machine. They all need freshly ground, fine coffee, and most need a good grinder to do well; see our espresso grinder guide and manual grinder guide. If you want a conventional machine instead, see espresso machines under $300 and espresso machines for beginners.

Two cautions from the sources. First, neither source gives a bar rating for most models, and HomeCoffeeExpert notes espresso generally needs at least 9 bar, so treat "espresso" here as hand-built pressure whose result depends on your technique. Second, nearly every model has a learning curve: HomeCoffeeExpert reports about 8 to 12 shots to dial in on the ones it gives a number for (Pixapresso about 10, Leverpresso about 12, Flair 2GO about 8, Kompresso about 10).

The espresso makers

Wacaco Pixapresso

HomeCoffeeExpert's top pick: a self-heating battery unit that takes ground coffee in 8 g or 16 g baskets (pressurized or not) or Nespresso-style pods, with pre-infusion, adjustable temperature and a leak-proof lid and cup. Its author reports about 10 shots to dial in and one result of 16 g in, 55 ml of water at 92°C, about 32 g out in 26 seconds. Listed cons: slow to heat, a janky touchscreen, a cheap-feeling tamper and funnel, heavier than rivals, and it scratches easily.

Give it a look if you want the fewest steps on the road and can pay the most. Check price

HUGH Leverpresso Lite V4

A hand lever with a 51 mm portafilter, 560 g and 20 cm tall, with no heater, so you need a kettle. HomeCoffeeExpert gives it 9/10, reports about 12 attempts to dial in (18 g in, 36 g out in 30 seconds, using water 90 seconds off the boil) and lists full manual control and easy cleanup as strengths. Cons: a steep learning curve, it needs a flat dry surface, it is best for one person, and it struggles with light roasts.

Give it a look if you enjoy the process and drink medium to dark roasts. Check price

Wacaco Picopresso

350 g, a 52 mm naked portafilter, an 18 g dose, a sturdy build and a travel case, with cleanup under five minutes per the source (8.5/10). Cons it lists: the pump needs grip strength, the body is hot to touch, it takes practice, it cannot heat water or take pods, and the WDT tool is flimsy. A separate search summary we could not verify calls it the top portable option; we do not rely on that.

Give it a look if you camp or travel and want real portafilter espresso in a small package. Check price

OutIn Nano

A self-heating battery model (about 2.5 to 4 minutes to heat, per the source) taking pods or grounds. Cons: slow recharge, the battery drains in 3 to 5 shots when heating water (never more than 4 in HomeCoffeeExpert's testing), it can jam and is messy with grounds, and a $40 Basket Plus kit is needed to reach 16–18 g doses. Rated 8/10.

Give it a look if you want easy pod coffee on hikes. Check price

Flair 2GO

A lever machine with a pressure gauge that folds into a travel case, and the heaviest here at 1.8 kg. Pros: barista-quality shots, full control, easy cleanup. Cons: large, needs space and a full setup, steep learning curve, physical strength required, and a temperature-controlled kettle is recommended. HomeCoffeeExpert reports about 8 shots to dial in and rates it 8/10, best for RV or van owners.

Give it a look if you travel by vehicle, not on foot. Check price

Cafflano Kompresso

The lightest at 178 g, with a naked portafilter, hot or cold espresso, and a durable body. HomeCoffeeExpert (7.5/10) tested a 15 g dose aiming for about 30 g out, and lists shots of up to 8 minutes, a flimsy feel, a poor tamper and a demand for a precise grind. It is the budget-tier option at under $100 per the source.

Give it a look if weight and cost matter most and you will tinker. Check price

Staresso Classic

A top-mounted pump unit taking pods or grounds, 400 g, with a thick cup included (7/10). Pros: lightweight, simple to clean, good value ("well under $100"). Cons: the tamper is too small for the basket, the pump is loud and sluggish, and it is hard to stabilize while pumping.

Give it a look if you want an affordable occasional-use option. Check price

Flair Neo, Pro and 58

Brew Coffee at Home's 2023 comparison (no formal test method described) puts the Neo as the affordable, no-electricity starter: a pressurized portafilter forgives coarser grinds but, per the author, creates a synthetic crema and limits shot quality. The Pro adds a pressure gauge, a case, a metal tamper and a bottomless 45 mm portafilter, at nearly double the Neo's price, and wants a premium grinder and dialing-in experience. The 58 adds an electrically heated group head and a lockable 58 mm portafilter at a significantly higher price, and the source covers it only briefly. The page lists the Neo's chamber as both "60 grams" and 60 ml, and is nearly three years old, so check the current lineup.

Give them a look if you want a counter-top lever setup with room to upgrade. Check price

Which should you buy?

  • Backpacking, lowest weight: Cafflano Kompresso (178 g) or Wacaco Picopresso (350 g).
  • Hotel or road trip with minimal fuss: Pixapresso or OutIn Nano (built-in heater).
  • RV or van with space: Flair 2GO.
  • Home counter, learning espresso: Flair Neo to start; Pro or Leverpresso for more control.
  • Tightest budget: Staresso Classic or Kompresso.
  • Budget for a grinder: all of these assume one. A hand grinder that can go fine is the usual pairing.

FAQ

Do manual espresso makers make real espresso?

That depends on your technique and grind. HomeCoffeeExpert says espresso needs at least 9 bar generally and gives no bar rating for most of these, so expect shots similar to espresso rather than a guarantee. Models with a pressurized basket (the Neo, and optional baskets on some others) are more forgiving but, per Brew Coffee at Home, make crema that is partly synthetic.

Do I need a kettle?

Yes for every model except the Pixapresso and OutIn Nano, which heat water from a battery (and the Flair 58, which heats electrically).

Can I use pre-ground coffee?

Fine grind consistency is the main complaint across the sources (the Kompresso needs "a precise grind"), so pre-ground drip coffee is unlikely to work. A fine setting on a burr grinder is the usual route.

How is this different from an AeroPress?

An AeroPress makes a concentrated coffee at lower pressure; see our AeroPress, French press and moka pot comparison and travel coffee maker guide.
