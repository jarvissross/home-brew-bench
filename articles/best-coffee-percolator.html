A percolator cycles hot water up a central tube and drips it back over the grounds until the pot is as strong as you want. It is old technology that still has a following for camping, big batches and a hot, strong cup. It is also easy to over-extract. This guide compares the stovetop and electric models named in the two roundups and says where the sources disagree. If you want a cleaner cup from a machine, see our drip coffee maker guide; for another stovetop brewer, our moka pot guide.

How percolating works, and what it costs you

  • Boiling water over the same grounds. Home Grounds warns that percolators need boiling water, which risks over-extraction and a burnt or bitter taste, though some drinkers like the smoky result. Watching the perk rate through a clear top helps you adjust heat on stovetop models; the page gives no fixed brew time, and says the 12-cup Farberware took about 10 minutes.
  • Grind coarse. Home Grounds says pre-ground coffee for a percolator should be coarser than usual because the metal basket lets fine grounds through, and that freshly ground beans taste better. Paper filters are an option for less sediment. If your grinder cannot go coarse, see our grinder explainer.
  • Medium roast. Home Grounds recommends medium roast; dark can taste bitter and light can taste watery or astringent.
  • Sediment and cholesterol. Metal-basket brewing leaves more coffee oils in the cup than paper. We cover the cafestol research in our reusable filters guide; we found no study testing percolators specifically.
  • Not for a single mug. One roundup we read says that if you drink one mug and prefer clarity, a drip brewer makes a cleaner cup. Electric percolators also did badly below about half capacity in Home Grounds' notes.

What we'd skip or double-check

  • Glass-body stovetop pots: CCS reports lids melting and a plastic-filter taste on the CAFE BREW; we found little else on it.
  • Mixpresso electric: CCS reports components breaking down quickly.
  • GSI enamelware 8-cup: CCS says it often fails to percolate properly.

FAQ

How long does a percolator take?

Home Grounds gives no fixed time for stovetop models and says the 12-cup Farberware took about 10 minutes. Follow the maker's instructions.

Can I use a paper filter in a percolator?

Home Grounds mentions paper filters as an option for less sediment. Check that your model has room for one.

Why does my percolator coffee taste bitter?

Home Grounds points to boiling and over-extraction, dark roasts and too-fine grounds. Try a coarser grind, a medium roast and shorter perking.

Are percolators safe on induction or glass-top stoves?

We did not find reliable compatibility information for the models above. Check each listing before buying; CCS notes the glass CAFE BREW needs a metal adapter on electric stoves.
