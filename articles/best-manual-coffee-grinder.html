A hand grinder is the cheapest way to get good burrs. With no motor to pay for, a $100 hand grinder can carry larger burrs than many electric grinders at twice the price. The tradeoff is effort: you crank every dose yourself, and most hand grinders hold enough for one or two cups.

This guide compares eight hand grinders that show up again and again in 2026 manual grinder guides, from a $59 ceramic-burr starter to the $325+ Comandante. The biggest differences between them are burr material and size, how fine each click is (which decides whether espresso is practical), capacity, and how portable they are. If you'd rather not crank, see our electric grinders under $200 and grinders for pour-over guides.

What to look for in a hand grinder

1. Steel or ceramic burrs

Ceramic burrs (Skerton Pro, Porlex) keep prices low and don't rust. A Skerton Pro review reports weaker consistency than other hand grinders, especially at coarse settings, and a 2026 guide notes that the Porlex's ceramic burrs can chip. Every grinder from the Q Air up uses stainless steel burrs, which is where most 2026 guides start their recommendations.

2. Burr size

Larger burrs are generally described as grinding faster, because each turn of the handle cuts more coffee. The 48mm burrs in the K6, J-Ultra and K-Ultra are the largest here. The Q Air (38–40mm), the C3 (38mm) and the Comandante (39mm) are smaller. The Comandante shows that burr size isn't everything: its reputation rests on burr quality and build rather than diameter.

3. Step size decides espresso

Espresso needs tiny adjustments. The J-Ultra's roughly 8 microns per click is built for that. The K-Ultra (20) and K6 (about 16) can do espresso as well. At about 30 microns, the standard Comandante is mainly a filter grinder unless you add the Red Clix axle. For pour-over, French press and AeroPress, any grinder here has fine enough steps.

4. Capacity and portability

Travel grinders like the Q Air (15–18 g) and Porlex Mini II (about 20 g) hold one cup and weigh little. The 48mm grinders hold 30–40 g, enough for two cups, but weigh roughly 630–700 g. If you brew for more than two people every morning, a hand grinder gets tiring. Consider electric.

5. External or internal adjustment

The K6, K-Ultra and J-Ultra adjust with a numbered ring on the outside, so you can switch between brew methods without taking the grinder apart. The Q Air's dial is inside, under the handle, which reviewers call less convenient. The Skerton Pro adjusts with a knob under the burrs.

FAQ

Is a manual grinder better than an electric one?

Not better, but cheaper for the same burr quality. With no motor to pay for, about $100 gets you 48mm steel burrs in a hand grinder (the KINGrinder K6). For comparison, The Coffee Locator lists 40mm burrs for the electric Baratza Encore ESP and 41mm for the Fellow Opus. Electric wins on convenience and batch size.

How long does it take to grind by hand?

It depends on the grinder, the grind size and the roast. Finer grinds and smaller burrs take longer, and larger burrs (48mm) cut more per turn. We haven't timed these grinders ourselves, so we don't quote a number. Expect espresso-fine doses to take noticeably longer than pour-over doses on any of them.

Can a hand grinder make espresso?

Yes, if its steps are fine enough. In this list the J-Ultra (about 8 microns per click) is built for it, and the K-Ultra and K6 can do it too. The standard Comandante needs the Red Clix axle. The ceramic-burr grinders aren't a good fit.

Ceramic or steel burrs?

Ceramic burrs are cheaper and don't rust, but a Skerton Pro review notes weaker consistency, and a 2026 guide notes the Porlex's ceramic burrs can chip. Steel burrs are standard on every grinder from about $69 up.
