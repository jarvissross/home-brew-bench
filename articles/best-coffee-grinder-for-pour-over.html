Pour-over (V60, Kalita Wave, Chemex, Origami and similar drippers) is forgiving compared with espresso. You don't need an espresso-grade grinder. What you do need is a grinder that makes even, medium-fine grounds and lets you move in small, repeatable steps, because the grind is the main thing you adjust when a cup tastes sour or bitter.

This guide compares seven grinders that appear again and again in 2026 pour-over buying guides, at three budget levels: under $150, around $200–$260, and the $400 filter-only specialist. Four are electric and three are hand grinders. If you also want espresso, see our grinders under $200 guide, which covers espresso-capable models.

What matters for pour-over

1. Even grounds in the medium-fine range

Pour-over sits roughly in the middle of the grind range. Seattle Coffee Gear's Encore guide, for example, suggests settings 13–14 for a V60-style cone and 20–21 for a Chemex. Fines (dust) slow the drawdown and add bitterness, and boulders under-extract. A better burr set makes fewer of both, and that is most of what you pay for as prices rise.

2. Small, repeatable steps

You'll adjust a notch or two at a time. Stepped dials with clear numbers make it easy to return to a setting. Hand grinders like the K6 and K-Ultra state their step size (16 and 20 microns per click), which makes it easy to share and repeat recipes.

3. Conical or flat burrs

Coffee guides commonly describe flat burrs as producing a more uniform grind and a "clearer" cup, and conical burrs as more forgiving and with more body. In this list only the Fellow Ode Gen 2 uses flat burrs. Treat this as a general tendency, not a rule. Burr quality and alignment matter as much as shape.

4. Electric or hand grinder

For the same money, a hand grinder usually gets you larger or better burrs. The 48mm burrs in the K6 and K-Ultra are bigger than the 40mm burrs in every electric conical grinder here. The tradeoff is cranking 15–30 g by hand for every cup, and the small capacity. If you brew for two or more people every morning, buy electric.

The grinders in detail

Baratza Encore: best value electric

The Encore is the default beginner recommendation in many pour-over guides. It has 40mm steel conical burrs and 40 stepped settings, which a retailer puts at about 250–1200 microns, and a 225 g hopper per Baratza's spec sheet. It costs $149.95 at Seattle Coffee Gear. Its weak spot is espresso, which doesn't matter for pour-over.

Pros
  • Published starting settings for V60 (13–14) and Chemex (20–21)
  • Simple controls, large hopper
  • Lowest-priced electric grinder here
Cons
  • Reviewers call it loud
  • Fewer fine-tuning options than the Opus or hand grinders

Buy it if you want a reliable electric grinder for pour-over, drip and French press at the lowest price. Check price

Fellow Opus: best electric under $200 for fine-tuning

The Opus has 40mm 6-blade conical burrs, 41+ stepped settings with a printed grind guide, and a micro-adjust ring inside the grinder for finer steps between settings. Whole Latte Love lists it at $195.00. Its capacity is 110 g, enough for a cup or two at a time.

Pros
  • Micro-adjust ring for small pour-over tweaks
  • Compact, design-forward body
  • Retailer lists a 2-year warranty
Cons
  • Small 110 g capacity
  • Reviewers report mixed results from its anti-static feature

Buy it if you want finer adjustment than the Encore without going much over $200. Check price

Baratza Virtuoso+: best step-up electric with a timer

The Virtuoso+ uses 40mm stainless conical burrs at a slow 550 RPM, which Whole Latte Love says reduces heat, noise and static. It has 40 stepped settings (about 200–1200 microns per the retailer), and a digital timer up to 40 seconds in 0.1-second steps with a pulse mode. Whole Latte Love lists a $249.95 regular price and a $223.96 sale price, but the grinder was sold out there when we checked. Clive Coffee also lists $249.95. Listings disagree on hopper size (8 oz vs 300 g).

Pros

  • Slower burr speed than many entry grinders
  • Baratza-serviced 1-year warranty (per retailer)
Cons
  • About $100 more than the Encore for the same burr size
  • Some listings note static

Its listed recommended grinds are cold brew, French press, drip, pour-over and AeroPress-style filters. Espresso isn't on the list. It costs $399.95 at both Whole Latte Love and Clive Coffee.

Pros

The K6 puts 48mm stainless heptagonal conical burrs, the same burr size as the K-Ultra, into a grinder that review sites price at about $100–$110. Sources disagree on some details: one review gives 12–15 microns per click and a larger capacity, so check the listing. The Coffee Locator's review (updated October 14, 2026) praises its numbered ring for repeatable V60 settings, and notes it's heavy for travel and has a single bearing.

  • 48mm burrs at a price close to the C3
  • External numbered ring for easy, repeatable settings
Cons

Buy it if you want the biggest burrs you can get for about $100 and don't mind hand grinding. Check price

1Zpresso K-Ultra: best premium hand grinder

1Zpresso's own page describes a heptagonal conical burr, an external ring with 100 clicks per rotation, and 20 microns per click, and lists pour-over and espresso among its uses. In a side-by-side comparison, Barista Life described the K-Ultra's pour-over as lighter-bodied with more clarity than the K6.

FAQ

What grind size is best for pour-over?

Medium-fine, often compared to table salt. Start with the grinder maker's or retailer's suggested setting (for example, 13–14 on the Encore for a V60), then adjust by taste. Grind finer if the coffee tastes sour or thin, and coarser if it tastes bitter or the drawdown is slow.

Do I need a $400 grinder for pour-over?

No. Coffee guides consistently point out that pour-over is far less demanding than espresso. The Encore or a 48mm hand grinder gets you most of the way. The Ode Gen 2's flat burrs are a refinement for people who already brew pour-over daily.

Is a hand grinder good enough for pour-over?

Yes. For one or two cups, a hand grinder often has larger burrs than an electric grinder at the same price. The cost is time and effort for each dose.

Can I use a blade grinder for pour-over?

You can, but coffee guides explain that blade grinders leave a mix of dust and chunks. Pour-over is sensitive to that, and the result is often a stalled drawdown and uneven taste. Even the cheapest grinder here is a big upgrade. See our burr vs blade explainer.
