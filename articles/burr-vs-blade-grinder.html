Short answer: a blade grinder chops beans with a spinning blade, so you get a mix of fine dust and large chunks. A burr grinder crushes beans between two burrs set a fixed distance apart, so the grounds come out much more even. Every source we read agrees that even grounds brew a more balanced cup. A blade grinder is still workable for drip, French press and cold brew, but it can't make espresso.

The catch is price. The Coffee Locator puts blade grinders at $15–$30 (the two we list below were $21.99–$35.02 at the retailers we checked). Burr grinders in our guides start at $58.50 for a hand grinder, and our top all-round electric pick, the Baratza Encore, was $149.95. This guide explains the difference, shows which brew methods really need burrs, and gives you ways to get more from a blade grinder if you're not ready to upgrade.

How each grinder works

Blade grinders

A blade grinder is a small motor with a propeller-style blade at the bottom of a cup. Seattle Coffee Gear's guide puts it simply: the blade "doesn't measure anything." It keeps chopping whatever it hits for as long as you hold the button, so some pieces get chopped into dust while others stay large. There's no setting for grind size, so you control coarseness only by how long you run it.

Burr grinders

A burr grinder feeds beans between two hard, ridged surfaces called burrs. One spins and one stays still. Beans get crushed until the pieces are small enough to fall through the gap between them, so the gap sets the grind size. Move the burrs closer for a finer grind, and further apart for a coarser one. Because every particle has to pass the same gap, the grounds come out far more even, and a setting you liked yesterday gives you the same grind today.

Conical vs flat burrs

Burr grinders come in two shapes. Conical burrs (a cone inside a ring) are what you'll find in almost every grinder under $200, including the Baratza Encore, Fellow Opus and most hand grinders. Seattle Coffee Gear describes them as slower-running, lower-heat, low-retention and forgiving to dial in. Flat burrs (two parallel rings) are described as producing a very uniform grind and a clear cup, which is why filter-focused grinders like the Fellow Ode Gen 2 use them. Seattle Coffee Gear calls both "dramatically better than any blade grinder" and says the difference between them is more nuanced than most buyers need to worry about.

Why grind consistency changes the taste

Brewing coffee is extraction: water dissolves flavor out of the grounds. Small particles give up their flavor fast, and large ones give it up slowly. When every particle is about the same size, they all finish extracting at about the same time. When the grind is a mix of dust and chunks, the dust over-extracts and tastes bitter while the chunks under-extract and taste sour. Both The Coffee Locator and Prima Coffee note that a blade-ground cup can taste bitter and sour at the same time. Prima calls the mix "fines" (very small particles) and "boulders" (very large ones).

The Coffee Locator adds that pulse grinding trims some of the fines but leaves the coarse chunks, so it narrows the gap without closing it. It also describes blade-ground pour-over as "muddier" and blade-ground French press as having more sludge.

Does heat matter?

You'll often read that blade grinders "burn" coffee. The sources we trust are more measured. The Coffee Locator says long blade runs warm the grounds, especially the fines, which can dull aroma, but it calls heat a smaller factor than particle size. Seattle Coffee Gear only says that conical burrs run slower and make less heat. We found no reliable measured temperature comparison, so we don't quote numbers. Consistency is the real reason to upgrade.

Already own a blade grinder? Five ways to get more from it

These tips come from The Coffee Locator's guide. They narrow the gap, but they don't close it.

  1. Don't overfill. Fill it no more than about a third so the beans can move.
  2. Pulse, don't hold. Grind in short bursts of a few seconds instead of holding the button down.
  3. Shake between pulses. Shaking or tilting the grinder moves big pieces back into the blade.
  4. Sift out the dust. A fine sieve removes the powder that causes bitterness and sludge.
  5. Pick a forgiving brew method. French press, drip and cold brew tolerate an uneven grind better than pour-over. Espresso won't work.

FAQ

Is a burr grinder really worth it?

If you brew pour-over or espresso, yes. Those methods are the most sensitive to uneven grounds, and espresso is impossible with a blade. For an auto-drip machine or French press, the upgrade is smaller but still noticeable according to the guides we read. The cheapest real burr option is a hand grinder around $60.

Do blade grinders burn coffee?

Long runs warm the grounds, and one guide says that can dull aroma. But the same guide calls heat a smaller problem than uneven particle size. Pulsing in short bursts limits both.

Can I use a blade grinder for espresso?

No. Espresso needs a very fine, very even grind and small adjustments between settings. A blade grinder can't do either.

How long do burr grinders last?

The Coffee Locator says steel burrs can last many years of daily use and that ceramic burrs can chip if a stone gets through. It also notes that on a blade grinder the motor usually fails first. Prima Coffee says a Baratza Encore "should last you longer than five years with good maintenance."

Is a manual burr grinder better than an electric blade grinder?

For grind consistency, the guides we read all favor burrs, hand-cranked or not. The tradeoff is effort and batch size: a hand grinder holds enough for one or two cups. See our manual grinder guide.
